# 1. Concepts

## A scene is a document

A-Frame's central idea: a 3D scene is written as HTML. Not generated by HTML, not configured through HTML — it *is* HTML, in the same document tree as the rest of your page.

```html
<a-scene>
  <a-box position="-1 0.5 -3" color="#4CC3D9"></a-box>
</a-scene>
```

That's a complete, working 3D scene. Everything lives inside `<a-scene>`, which sets up the WebGL renderer, the VR entry point, and the headset connection on your behalf.

Because it's real HTML in a real document, everything you already know still applies: you can select entities with `document.querySelector`, inspect them in devtools, and edit attributes live and watch the scene respond.

## Entities and components

Under the hood A-Frame uses an **entity-component-system** pattern, which sounds academic but is simple in practice:

- An **entity** is a thing in the scene. On its own it's empty — a position in space with nothing attached.
- A **component** gives an entity a behaviour or an appearance. `geometry` gives it shape, `material` gives it a surface, `light` makes it emit light, `look-controls` makes it follow your mouse.

An entity is written `<a-entity>`, and components are its attributes:

```html
<a-entity geometry="primitive: box" material="color: #4CC3D9" position="0 1 -3"></a-entity>
```

The mental model that makes this click: **entities are nouns, components are adjectives and verbs.** A thing, and what's true about it.

## Primitives are shorthand

Writing `geometry="primitive: box"` every time would be miserable, so A-Frame ships **primitives** — convenience tags that expand into an entity with components pre-attached. These two lines produce exactly the same thing:

```html
<a-entity geometry="primitive: box" material="color: #4CC3D9"></a-entity>
<a-box color="#4CC3D9"></a-box>
```

`<a-box>` is not a different kind of object. It's the same entity, spelled more conveniently. This matters later: when you want something a primitive doesn't offer, you drop to `<a-entity>` and attach components yourself, and nothing about the scene changes structurally.

## Why declarative matters here

Most 3D tools are imperative: you write instructions that build a scene step by step, or click through an interface that does it for you. A-Frame is declarative — you describe the end state and the framework works out how to produce it.

For learning, that has one enormous advantage: **describing a scene and coding it become the same act.** "There's a red sphere floating above a green floor, under a dark sky" translates almost word for word into tags. There's no translation layer between the idea and the implementation, which is exactly why a workshop can get a complete beginner to a finished scene in two hours.

This is the "Description" step of the workshop's [4 Ds](../overview.md), and it's the part that surprises people most.

## The scene graph

Entities nest, and nesting means attachment. A child's position is relative to its parent, not to the world:

```html
<a-entity position="0 1 -5">        <!-- a table, 5 metres away -->
  <a-box position="0 0.5 0"></a-box> <!-- a cup, half a metre above the TABLE -->
</a-entity>
```

Move the parent and the child moves with it. This is how the camera rig in the starter template works — the camera is a child of a rig entity, so moving the rig moves your viewpoint without touching the camera's own settings.
