A 2-hour hands-on workshop

Spatial Storytelling

Designing your first WebXR scene

Don't start with tooling. Start with the promise: in two hours everyone in this room will have a 3D scene of their own, live on the internet, that they can send to someone. No installs, no prior experience.

By the end of today

Viewable in any browser. In VR too, if there's a headset around.

Say the deliverable out loud, plainly. Most of the room assumes 3D means Unity, a degree, and six months. Undercutting that assumption early is the single most useful thing you do in the first ten minutes.

How we'll work

DirectionDecide the mood before writing anything.
DescriptionWrite tags that describe the scene.
DiscernmentChoose assets that serve the mood.
DelegationLet A-Frame handle the hard parts.
There's a script for this in the facilitator guide you can use verbatim. The short version: four moves, in order. Intent first, then description, then judgment, and the framework carries the complexity throughout.

Direction

Intent comes first

What does this space feel like?

Not "what objects will I place." That comes after.

The most common failure mode in a short build session is opening the editor with no idea what you're making and filling the time with random props. Deciding the mood first is what prevents that.

Description

Describing the scene is the code

This is not a metaphor. Each tag is a thing in the room.

<a-box>     is a box
<a-sphere>  is a sphere
<a-sky>     is the sky around everything
<a-light>   is a light source
Worth dwelling on. In most 3D tools you build a scene by clicking through an interface; here you write down what's there. That maps onto how people already describe spaces in language.

Discernment

Choosing, not collecting

  • A curated asset kit is ready to go
  • You can source your own — 10 minutes maximum
  • Free-to-use only: CC0, or CC-BY with credit
Enforce the cap out loud, now, before build time. Asset hunting is the single biggest time sink in the session and it will eat the whole hour if you let it. The skill being practised is judgment, not search.

Delegation

What you never have to write

  • The camera and head tracking
  • Mouse-look and keyboard movement
  • Lighting maths and rendering
  • The entire WebXR headset handshake

Naming a component is enough.

Point at look-controls and wasd-controls in the file. Each of those would be hundreds of lines by hand. Writing the name is the whole integration. That's what frees the hour up for composition.

Four attributes do most of the work

position="X Y Z"    where it is        (metres)
rotation="X Y Z"    how it's turned    (degrees)
scale="X Y Z"       how big            (1 = normal)
color="#RRGGBB"     what colour

In context: the objects section →

Live-edit one colour value on the projector right now and let them watch it reload. That single moment does more than any explanation — it shows the loop is instant and nothing can be permanently broken.
0:10 — 0:25

Negative Z is forward

You start at 0 0 0 looking down −Z.

Anything you want to see goes at a negative Z.
Everyone trips on this once. Better now than at minute fifty.

Call this out deliberately. When someone says "I added a box and nothing happened," this is the reason nine times out of ten — it's behind them. Teaching them to turn around is the fix.
0:10 — 0:25

Two lights, doing two jobs

<a-light type="ambient"     intensity="0.4"></a-light>
<a-light type="directional" intensity="0.8" position="-1 2 1"></a-light>

Ambient stops anything going black. Directional makes things look solid.

starter-template/index.html#L128-L129 →

Best live demo in the deck: set the directional intensity to 0 and reload. The cube flattens into a hexagon in front of them. Then put it back. Also warn them: the moment you add your own light, A-Frame's default lighting switches off. That surprises people who add one light and find their scene got darker.
0:10 — 0:25

Bringing in a model

<a-entity
  gltf-model="../asset-kit/models/lantern.glb"
  position="0 0 -4"
  scale="1 1 1"></a-entity>

Models are almost never the size you expect. Fix scale first.

Set expectations here: a model appearing enormous or invisibly tiny is normal, not a mistake. Scale is the first thing to reach for, not a sign something is broken.
0:25 — 0:35

Direction

Five minutes. No keyboards.

Pick a mood, a theme, or a one-sentence concept for your scene.

Somewhere abandoned. A place that's too quiet. A memory of a room. Underwater. The last shop open at 3am.

Hold this slide up for the full five minutes. Quiet, individual, no typing. Don't let it run long — you want a decision, not a polished concept. If someone is stuck at four minutes, tell them to pick the worst idea on the list and start; it will change once they build anyway.
0:35 — 1:35

Build time

One hour.

Your Direction drives every decision from here.

This is the bulk of the session and your job changes: stop presenting and start circulating. Most of the real teaching happens one-on-one in this hour.
0:35 — 1:35

What a finished scene needs

  • A sky or skybox
  • At least one light, set deliberately
  • Two or three objects placed with intention
  • It loads and enters VR from your shared link

That's the floor, not the ceiling.

Leave this one up on the projector during build time if you have a second screen. It answers "am I done?" without anyone having to ask.
0:35 — 1:35

If you get stuck

  • Nothing renders? Check the console, and the closing </a-scene>
  • No VR button? You're not on HTTPS or localhost
  • Model won't load? Needs to be a real .glb file, not a viewer page
  • Can't find the object? It's probably behind you
These four cover nearly every question you'll get. Full list with fixes is in the facilitator guide under "common failure points." Running short on time? Steer people to the minimum viable scene: primitives only, no custom models. Still complete, still shareable.
1:35 — 1:50

Swap links

Everyone already has a live URL. Go and stand in two or three other people's worlds.

This block matters more than it looks. Seeing someone else's scene in your own browser is what makes it land that they built something real and public, not a classroom exercise. If a headset is in the room, this is when it goes round.
1:50 — 2:00

One thing you'd add with more time

Go round the room. One sentence each.

Ends the session on intent rather than on what didn't get finished — which is the right note, because everyone runs out of time. Two questions worth collecting: what would you add, and where did you get stuck? The second one is what improves the next run of this workshop.
